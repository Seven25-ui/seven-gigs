How to Write a Simple Project Scope Document (With a Free Template)

Oct 26, 2026 · 4 min read

A scope document is a short written agreement about what you will do, and what you won't. It is the best protection against the most common freelance problems: extra requests, misunderstandings, and arguments about payment.

You don't need to be a lawyer to write one. A clear, one-page summary is often enough. In this tutorial we'll build one step by step.

Note: this is general information, not legal advice. For large or risky projects, consider asking a qualified professional to review your agreement. On platforms like Upwork, also keep your agreement inside the platform's contract and messages.

Why you need one

1. The client knows exactly what they are paying for.
2. You are protected from endless "small" extra requests.
3. You can point to the document when there is a disagreement.
4. It shows you are professional, and clients often feel safer because of it.

Step 1: Write a short project summary

Start with two or three sentences that describe the project and its goal.

Example: "Build a five-page website for a small bakery so customers can see the menu and contact the shop. The goal is to get more phone and email orders."

Step 2: List the deliverables

Deliverables are the exact things you will hand over. Be specific.

1. Five pages: Home, Menu, About, Contact, Gallery
2. Mobile-friendly design
3. A contact form that sends messages to the client's email
4. Setup on the client's hosting

Vague words like "a nice website" cause arguments. Specific items don't.

Step 3: State what is not included

This is the most important step, and the one most people skip. Write down what is outside the project.

1. Writing the text and taking photos for the website
2. Online payments or a shop
3. Logo design
4. Ongoing maintenance after delivery

If the client wants these later, you can offer them as separate work.

Step 4: Set a timeline

Break the project into stages with dates, for example:

1. Design preview: by day 3
2. First full version: by day 7
3. Revisions and final delivery: by day 10

Remember that your timeline depends on the client sending what you need, like text and images. Say so in the document.

Step 5: Define revisions

Say how many rounds of changes are included. For example, "Two rounds of revisions are included. Extra rounds are charged at an hourly rate." Without a limit, revisions can go on forever.

Step 6: Explain payment

Write the total price or rate and when you get paid. On Upwork, this usually means milestones. Match your milestones to your stages so you are paid as the work moves forward.

Step 7: Describe how to handle changes

New ideas will come, and that's normal. Agree on a simple rule: "Anything outside this document will be estimated separately, and work will begin after both sides agree."

Step 8: Ask the client to confirm

Send the document and ask for a clear reply, such as "Please reply 'Agreed' if this matches what you expect, or tell me what to change." Wait for the answer before you start.

A template you can copy

Replace the parts in brackets with your own details:

PROJECT SCOPE: [Project name]

1. Summary
[Two or three sentences about the project and its goal.]

2. Deliverables
- [Deliverable one]
- [Deliverable two]
- [Deliverable three]

3. Not included
- [Item one]
- [Item two]

4. Timeline
- [Stage one]: by [date]
- [Stage two]: by [date]
- Final delivery: by [date]
Dates depend on receiving the needed materials from the client on time.

5. Revisions
[Number] rounds of revisions are included. Extra rounds: [rate].

6. Payment
[Total price or rate]. Milestones: [list them].

7. Changes
Anything outside this document will be estimated separately and begins only after both sides agree.

8. Communication
Updates: [how often and where]. Reply time: [for example, within 24 hours on weekdays].

Copy it into a notes app, and adapt it for each project.

Common mistakes

1. Writing too much. Long documents don't get read.
2. Using vague words like "simple", "quick", or "etc."
3. Forgetting to list what is not included
4. Starting work before the client confirms
5. Never updating the scope when the project changes

The bottom line

A good scope document is short, specific, and confirmed in writing. It protects your time and your client's budget, and it makes the whole project calmer. Write one before every job, and you'll avoid most of the arguments that new freelancers dread.
