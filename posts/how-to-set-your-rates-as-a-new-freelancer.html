How to Set Your Rates as a New Freelancer

Oct 26, 2026 · 4 min read

Setting your first rate is one of the hardest parts of freelancing. Charge too little and you burn out. Charge too much with no proof and clients scroll past you.

The good news is that you don't have to guess. You can work out a fair starting point with a few simple steps.

Step 1: Start with what you need to earn

Write down how much money you need each month. Include your living costs, your savings goal, and some extra for slow months. A freelancer doesn't get paid for sick days, holidays, or the time spent finding clients, so don't forget those.

Step 2: Count your real working hours

You will not bill eight hours a day. A big part of your time goes to finding jobs, writing proposals, answering messages, and learning new skills.

A realistic guess for a new freelancer is that only part of your working time can be billed to clients. Let's use an example: if you work 40 hours a week, maybe 15 to 25 of those hours are billable at the start.

Step 3: Do the simple math

Here is the formula:

Hourly rate = (monthly income you need) divided by (billable hours per month)

Example with made-up numbers: you want to earn 1,000 dollars a month and you expect about 80 billable hours. That gives 12.50 dollars per hour.

Now add a buffer. Platform fees and taxes take a share of what you earn, so your rate should be a little higher than the result of the formula. These numbers are only an example. Use your own costs and your own country's situation.

Step 4: Check the market

Look at what other freelancers with similar skills charge. On Upwork, search for freelancers who offer the same service and read their profiles. Look at their hourly rate, their job success, and how many jobs they have finished.

Don't copy the lowest price you see. Compare yourself with people at your own experience level, and note the range.

Step 5: Choose hourly or fixed price

1. Hourly works well when the scope is unclear or the work may grow. You get paid for the time you spend.
2. Fixed price works well when the project is clear and you can estimate it. You get paid for the result, and fast work earns you more per hour.

When you choose fixed price, write down exactly what is included. Then add a buffer for revisions and surprises.

Starting lower: a smart plan or a trap?

Many new freelancers start a little lower to win their first reviews. That can work, but only with a plan:

1. Decide in advance how long you will use the lower rate, for example your first three to five jobs.
2. Don't go so low that you can't afford the work.
3. Tell yourself the rate is temporary, and raise it as soon as you have reviews.

A rate that is far too low attracts clients who want a lot for very little, and they are often the hardest to work with.

How to raise your rates

1. After each good review, raise your rate a little.
2. Raise it when you are getting many invitations and your calendar is nearly full.
3. For long-term clients, give notice before the new rate starts, and explain the value you give them.

A client who is happy with your work will often accept a small increase. A client who walks away over a small increase may not have been a great fit.

What to say when a client asks for a discount

Stay polite and don't apologize for your price. You can say: "I'd be happy to work with your budget. Here is what I can do for that amount, and here is what would be added at my usual rate." This keeps the conversation friendly and shows the client what they get for their money.

The bottom line

A good rate covers your living costs, your time off, and your fees, and it leaves room to grow. Work out your numbers, check the market, start with a plan, and raise your price as your results improve. Your rate is part of your message: it tells clients how much you value your work.
