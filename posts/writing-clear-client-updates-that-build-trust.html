Writing Clear Client Updates That Build Trust

Oct 26, 2026 · 3 min read

Most client problems don't start with bad work. They start with silence. When a client doesn't hear from you, they begin to wonder: "Is anything happening? Did they forget me? Will this be late?"

A short, clear update answers all of that before the client has to ask. It takes only a few minutes, and it is one of the cheapest ways to look professional.

Why updates matter

1. They build trust, especially with a new client who doesn't know you yet.
2. They catch misunderstandings early, while they are still easy to fix.
3. They make your work visible. Clients can't see the hours you spent, but they can read a clear summary.
4. They create a written record of what was done and decided.

A simple four-part format

You don't need long messages. Use these four parts every time:

1. Done: what you finished since the last update
2. In progress: what you are working on now
3. Next: what comes after that, and when
4. Needed from you: anything you are waiting for from the client

Here is an example:

Hi Anna, here is today's update on your website.

Done: homepage layout and mobile menu are finished.
In progress: the contact page.
Next: the About page, then testing on different phones. I expect to send you a preview on Thursday.
Needed from you: your logo file in PNG or SVG format, please.

Thanks, Gene

It is short, easy to scan, and the client knows exactly what to do next.

How often should you send updates?

It depends on the project, but a good rule is to agree on a rhythm at the start. For example:

1. Small, fast projects: one update when you start and one when you deliver
2. Projects that last a week or two: an update every two or three days
3. Longer projects: a weekly summary, plus a message whenever something important changes

Ask the client what they prefer. Then keep your promise. A reliable schedule matters more than a perfect one.

Write for someone who is busy

1. Put the most important point in the first line.
2. Use short sentences and simple words.
3. Use a short list when you have several items.
4. Always say what you need from the client and by when.
5. Avoid technical terms the client doesn't know, or explain them in one line.

How to share bad news

Sometimes there will be a delay or a problem. Tell the client early, not on the deadline day. Use this order:

1. State the problem clearly and briefly.
2. Say what caused it, without blaming anyone.
3. Give your plan and a new date.
4. Ask for anything you need to fix it.

Example: "I need to let you know that the delivery will be one day late. The payment feature needed extra testing, and I want to be sure it works for your customers. I'll send the final version on Friday afternoon. Please let me know if that causes a problem."

Most clients accept honest news. What they don't forgive is a surprise.

Common mistakes

1. Sending long messages that nobody reads
2. Disappearing for days with no word
3. Sending only good news and hiding problems
4. Forgetting to say what you need from the client
5. Using messages to complain instead of to inform

A template you can copy

Replace the parts in brackets:

Hi [name], here is the update for [project].

Done: [what you finished]
In progress: [what you are doing now]
Next: [what is coming, with a date]
Needed from you: [what you need, or "nothing right now"]

[Short closing line]

Save it in a notes app, and filling it in takes less than two minutes.

The bottom line

Clear updates are not extra work. They are part of the service. Keep them short, send them on a steady schedule, and be honest when things change. Clients who feel informed trust you more, argue less, and are far more likely to hire you again.
